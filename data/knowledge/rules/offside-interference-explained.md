## Being in a position is not the offence

The single most useful thing to understand about offside is that it has two halves, and the first half is harmless. A player can stand in an offside position for the entire match without breaking any rule; the position is simply where that player happens to be at the moment a teammate plays the ball. Nothing is decided until the second half, which asks whether the player then did something with it. So the question "was he offside?" is incomplete on its own, and the question that actually decides the case is whether that player became involved in active play.

## Where the line is drawn

The offside line comes from the second-last opponent, and by extension from the last opponent if the attacking player is level with them, because being level is not being ahead. In practice the judgement uses the part of the head, torso or feet that is nearest to the opponents' goal line, which is why a striker can be flagged while his chest appears level with a retreating defender. The arms are left out of that comparison. A goalkeeper who has come off the line becomes just another opponent in the arithmetic, and that is often where an offside trap quietly fails.

## Becoming involved in active play

The clearest way to turn a position into an offence is to play or touch a ball passed forward by a teammate. But the involvement test is broader than possession: a player in an offside position can commit the offence by making a play towards the ball when an opponent could have competed for it, or by obstructing a defender's path to it. This is why a striker who never touches the ball can still be flagged: a run that forces a defender to change what he was doing has already involved him. Practically, attackers in that position either commit fully or withdraw visibly.

## Interfering with an opponent without touching the ball

The question coaches ask most often is what makes it an offside offence if a player never touches the ball, and the answer usually involves the goalkeeper. Standing directly in the goalkeeper's line of sight at the moment of a shot, making a movement that clearly distracts a defender from playing the ball, or challenging for it in a way that changes what an opponent does, can all be enough. The reasoning is that an advantage was taken from where the player happened to be standing when the ball was played. Conversely, a player who is well wide of the ball's route and makes no movement towards it is normally left alone, because nothing about his position altered anybody's decision.

## Gaining an advantage after a rebound

Offside is judged at the moment the teammate plays the ball, and that timing matters when the ball comes back into the striker's path afterwards. A shot that rebounds off the goalframe, or a save that falls to an attacker who was ahead of the last defender when the shot was taken, is generally an offence, because the player gained an advantage from his position. The logic changes when the sequence is broken by an opponent's deliberate play, since that starts a new phase of judgement and resets who is onside. Distinguishing a rebound from a new play is the hardest part of the rule to learn.

## Deflections, deliberate plays and a brand new judgement

When a defender gets a foot to the ball, the question becomes whether that was a deliberate play or an uncontrollable deflection. A controlled pass or an attempted clearance under no real pressure tends to start the matter afresh, which is why a defence that tries to play its way out of pressure can hand the striker a legitimate position. A ball that squashes off a shin, or a scramble where nobody could reasonably have acted differently, does not reset anything, and the striker's original position still counts. The test officials apply is whether the opponent had time, clarity and choice.

## Restarts where offside does not apply

A handful of restarts cannot produce an offside offence no matter where the receiver stood when the ball was put down: a goal kick, a throw-in and a corner kick are the standard examples, which is why teams attack corners with runners starting deep. Offside also cannot be called on a player who is in his own half of the pitch, or on a player who is level rather than ahead. And once play restarts for a free kick the previous phase is finished; positions before the whistle do not carry over.

## Check the current wording

The laws of the game are written and maintained by the International Football Association Board, and this part of them has been adjusted and clarified more than once, usually after periods when interpretation had drifted. This document explains the structure and reasoning behind the rule in plain language for training and analysis use. It is not a substitute for the law. For any real decision, read the current official text together with the competition's own regulations, since those documents override what is written here, and tournament organisers can vary rules such as the use of video review or the treatment of marginal lines.
