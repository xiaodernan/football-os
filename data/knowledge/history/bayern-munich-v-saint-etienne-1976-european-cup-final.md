## The short answer

The 1975–76 European Cup final was played at Hampden Park, Glasgow, on 12 May 1976 at 20:15 BST — before 54,864 witnesses. Bayern München 1–0 Saint-Étienne. Goal: Franz Roth 57' — a Beckenbauer free-kick fed to the right channel, Roth picked out half-volley at the edge of the penalty area. Referee Károly Palotai (Hungary).

This was Bayern's third consecutive European Cup (1974, 1975, 1976) — matched the Real Madrid 1956–60 run only Real Madrid and Ajax (1971–73) had set. Beckenbauer was at his last European Cup — after this, he retired to North American soccer. Saint-Étienne — les Verts — went home crying; Jean Michel Larqué and the rest of that team never played in a final again and remain the unluckiest runner-ups of the era.

## How the match decided itself

The game's early exchanges were fireworks for the French: Bathenay hit the crossbar in the 34th minute, Maier beaten; Santini header from Sarramagna cross struck the same crossbar five minutes later. Saint-Étienne appeared unstoppable.

Bayern were not silent. In the earliest passes, Bernd Dürnberger set off on a 50-metre solo run, feeding Gerd Müller — disallowed for offside by Palotai.

After the break Bayern were more composed. In the 57th minute, Müller was fouled by Osvaldo Piazza — Palotai gave a free-kick to Bayern from 20 metres, just left of the penalty arc. Beckenbauer took the free kick himself, tipped the ball to Roth on the right of the arc, and Roth scored half-high into the left side of the goal. Saint-Étienne could only aim for the crossbars — twice — and leave Hampden empty-handed.

## What the final meant

This was Bayern's greatest hour — their third consecutive European Cup, second only to Real Madrid's 1956–1960 run. Beckenbauer ended his European Cup era here, after three. Dettmar Cramer completed the hat-trick of titles.

Saint-Étienne's defeat is French football's greatest "if only": two hits off the crossbar in nine minutes, one from Bathenay (34') and one from Jacques Santini (from Sarramagna's cross, ~39'), it was their night — until it wasn't. Their supporters called Hampden's bar the "poteaux carrés" — the square posts — and brought that phrase home to France for the rest of a generation.
