## The short answer

The 2002–03 UEFA Champions League final was played at Old Trafford, Manchester, on 28 May 2003 at 19:45 local time, watched by 62,315 people. It was the first all-Italian Champions League final, and the second all-same-country final in the competition's history (after the all-Spanish 2000 final). The match finished 0–0 after 120 minutes; extra time used the silver-goal format and produced no goal. AC Milan won the penalty shoot-out 3–2 and earned their sixth European Cup — nine years after their 1994 win. Filippo Inzaghi and Andriy Shevchenko led Milan's attack; Marcello Lippi's Juventus lacked Pavel Nedvěd, suspended for the final for a third booking. Markus Merk was the referee; Paolo Maldini was Player of the match and lifted the trophy as captain, exactly 40 years after his father Cesare had done the same for Milan in the 1963 final at Wembley.

## How the match was played

The final was a tactical chess match dominated by both defences. Milan had a goal disallowed for offside in the ninth minute when Rui Costa was found to have obstructed Buffon's view. Filippo Inzaghi went close at the end of the first half, his hard header instinctively saved by Buffon. Juventus' Igor Tudor left with an injury and Alessandro Birindelli replaced him. In the second half Lippi replaced the suspended-for-the-final Camoranesi (deployed only because Nedvěd could not play) with Antonio Conte, who struck the crossbar; Marcelo Zalayeta came on for Juventus late; Milan had introduced Massimo Ambrosini, Roque Júnior and Serginho, using all three substitution slots. At 95 minutes Roque Júnior left the pitch injured but Milan had no substitution left, so he remained on the field. No goal arrived in extra time, and the final went to penalties.

The shoot-out went as follows — Juventus first: David Trezeguet missed, Dida saving; Serginho scored the Milan reply and Birindelli scored Juventus's; Clarence Seedorf had his Milan effort saved by Buffon; Zalayeta missed, Dida saving; Paolo Montero missed, Dida saving again; Kakha Kaladze's Milan penalty was saved by Buffon; Alessandro Nesta scored for Milan; Alessandro Del Piero scored for Juventus to level at 2–2; Andriy Shevchenko scored the decisive penalty; Milan won 3–2.

## What the route told us

Milan entered the tournament through the third qualifying round — they had won the 2002 Coppa Italia (beating Roma in the final) but finished third in Serie A, behind champions Juventus and Inter — and came through 2–2 on aggregate against Slovan Liberec (1–0 at home, 1–2 away) on the away-goals rule. They won Group G with 12 points (ahead of Deportivo La Coruña on goal difference), recording two wins over Bayern Munich (2–1 away, 2–1 at home), a 4–0 win at Deportivo and a 2–1 home win over Lens, while losing 1–2 at Lens and at home to Deportivo. Second group stage Group C: Milan again won 12 points — 1–0 over Real Madrid at home, 1–0 over Borussia Dortmund away and 1–0 wins over Lokomotiv Moscow in both legs — ahead of Real Madrid on 11 points; their two losses came at Real Madrid (1–3) and at home to Borussia Dortmund (0–1). In the quarter-finals Milan knocked out Ajax 3–2 on aggregate (0–0 away, 3–2 at home), and then edged the all-Italian semi-final against Inter Milan on away goals: 0–0 at home, 1–1 away.

Juventus came in as the 2002–03 Serie A champions — their 27th league title — and had entered as Group E winners on 13 points (including a 5–0 win over Dynamo Kyiv and a 3–0 home win against Manchester United). In the second stage they finished Group D runners-up behind Manchester United (7 points) after losing 1–2 away at Old Trafford but winning 3–0 at home. Barcelona were beaten 3–2 on aggregate in the quarter-finals (1–1 in Turin, 2–1 a.e.t. away in Barcelona). In the semi-final Juventus beat Real Madrid 4–3 on aggregate — losing 1–2 away and winning 3–1 in Turin. Pavel Nedvěd picked up his third yellow card of the tournament in the second leg and was suspended for the final; Marcello Lippi turned to Mauro Camoranesi.

## What the final meant

For Milan it was the sixth European Cup — nine years after 1994 — and a validation that the club could rebuild its identity without the late-1980s stars. For Juventus it was a seventh final lost (previous wins 1985 and 1996 at Heysel and Rome; losses 1973, 1983, 1997, 1998) at the edge of a Serie A era that was at its most powerful. For the tournament, Old Trafford's first major European final opened the door for the English venues that came after it. For Italian football at large, an all-Italian final at Old Trafford was the high-water mark of the Serie A duopoly as European benchmark. And Paolo Maldini lifting the trophy inside Old Trafford, with his father having done the same in 1963, underlined the Maldini name as the most decorated thread in AC Milan's European history.
