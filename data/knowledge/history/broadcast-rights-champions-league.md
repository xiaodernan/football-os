## The short answer

UEFA sells the Champions League, Europa League and Conference League media rights itself, as a package per territory — meaning that every market across Europe negotiates separately with UEFA, usually in three-year cycles, and holds all rights in that territory for the length of the deal. No individual club may sell its own Champions League broadcasts. The broadcaster in one country is never the broadcaster next door, and ownership of the rights for a given season changes when the deal expires. In the 2018–2021 cycle, UK rights were carried by BT Sport under an exclusive agreement struck originally for the 2015–18 window and renewed thereafter, covering all matches including the finals; in Germany, DAZN took over most matches of the 2018–19 season and onwards, with ZDF showing the finals free-to-air.

## How the rights are sold

Every few years UEFA opens a tender per territory and awards the package to the highest compliant bidder. The price of the rights became a core pillar around 2015, when the 2015–18 cycles closed with multi-billion-euro figures across the continent. The broadcaster sells the rights as subscriptions, advertising and sponsorship slots; production is subject to UEFA's technical specifications; the match experience is broadly the same everywhere but is legally the licensee's property in each territory.

## What this answers

Who owns the television rights for the 2019 Champions League final: in the UK, the answer at the time of the match was BT Sport — rights holder through the 2015–21 contracts, the same cycle that carried the Tottenham–Liverpool final at Estadio Metropolitano on 1 June 2019. In Germany, the answer was DAZN for most matches, with ZDF showing the final free-to-air — the same Germany host. Rights for show matches — the final above all — differ territory by territory; Madrid-based commentators will not be audible in Liverpool and Liverpool commentators will not be in Barcelona, because every deal is a separate territory.

## What it teaches

The television rights are why even small-quality European football can pay its best players: the centrally negotiated pool redistributes across the continent, and the size of the bid decides the size of the distribution. When an individual match or an individual final is at the crest of the product, it is not the host stadium that profits from broadcasting it; the rights belong to the licensees who bought them in their own markets, sometimes a season or more in advance — and that is the whole architecture of the modern club competition.
