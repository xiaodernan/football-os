## How many sprint sessions a week is the wrong first question

Questions like how many sprint sessions per week, or how much running is too much, are really two separate questions: how fast total load rises, and whether recovery keeps pace. Two hard running days with good sleep beat four moderate days survived on six hours of broken sleep. Football already supplies a lot of load through matches and small-sided games, so the honest starting point is writing down what you actually do in a normal week before adding anything. Most players who plateau or keep niggling are not undertrained; they are under-recovered at a decent volume.

## Progressing weekly load in small steps

Change one variable at a time and keep the weekly increase modest. General coaching practice is to raise total weekly volume by a small amount, commonly in the region of five to ten percent, and to hold the load steady for a week every few weeks. Intensity is the more stressful variable, so add either more running or faster running, not both in the same week. Returning after a break means starting below where you left off and rebuilding frequency before length. Progression that ignores that is how two good weeks end in a strained muscle in week three.

## Recovery between sessions: what is actually worth doing

Most recovery is boring and works: enough food, carbohydrate around hard days, protein across the day, fluids, and sleep. Beyond that, the reliable levers are lowering the next session when the previous one was genuinely hard, and leaving real gaps instead of stacking hard days back to back. Ice baths, compression, contrast water work and massage feel good and may ease perceived soreness, but evidence that they change adaptation is mixed, and cold immersion straight after strength work is sometimes avoided because it may blunt some training signals. None of it recovers a week of four hours of sleep.

## Why sleep changes what the training is worth

Sleep is where training gets converted into adaptation, and short sleep makes hard sessions costlier in several ways: perceived effort rises, reaction and decision quality drop, mood worsens, and researchers have repeatedly linked shortened sleep with more illness and more niggles in athletes, though the size of that link varies between studies. Repair processes lean on sleep stages, and the hormonal balance tilts the wrong way when sleep is cut short. Most adults do better on a consistent seven to nine hours than on five short weekday nights and a weekend catch-up, and a fixed wind-down in a dark room beats any supplement.

## Signals that load is running ahead of recovery

Watch for a cluster rather than one bad day. Typical warning signs: resting heart rate above your own baseline for several mornings, sleep that arrives late or breaks at three in the morning, legs still heavy on day two, motivation dropping before a session, and first-touch quality falling when nothing else has changed. Niggles that migrate from place to place are usually a load signal rather than one weak muscle. The sensible response is to cut the next week's volume by about a quarter and keep the intensity short and clean, rather than stopping entirely or pushing through.

## A sample week and how to log it

For a fitted adult playing one match a week, a workable pattern is: day one easy movement; day two a hard session with sprints or a small-sided game; day three strength or repeated-acceleration work; day four a moderate technical session; day five short and sharp; match day; then a genuine low day. That is two to three quality days, which is plenty. Log four things weekly and nothing more: hours trained, hardest efforts attempted, average sleep, and a one-to-five readiness rating, because patterns show in writing better than in memory.

## Evidence and its limits

This is where I would rather be plain than confident. Popular load rules, including the idea that a specific week-to-week ratio reliably predicts injury, come from research findings that have been questioned and re-analysed, and later analyses suggest the signal is weaker and noisier than the original framing implied. Sleep and injury research is mostly observational, so it struggles to separate short sleep from long travel, congestion, stress or simply more training. Everything above is general practice offered as a plausible range rather than a measured threshold, and I have deliberately not attached a number to a study. Your own log beats any population average, and a plan shaped by a medical condition or a returning injury belongs with a qualified professional.

## When this does not apply

Weekly progression logic assumes a stable life outside training, and that is often untrue. For young players load should stay modest and mostly game-based, with no maximal sprint prescriptions and plenty of unstructured play; growth-related pain is a reason to speak to a medical professional, not to push through a plan. During a run of matches every three or four days, maintaining beats building, and the sensible week removes volume rather than adding it. After illness with fever, or any injury, restart below your previous level with professional guidance. And if your real constraint is food, work hours or caring duties rather than programme design, more structure will not fix it.
