## "my striker tore a hamstring in March — when can he play again"

Returning to play is the slowest decision in squad management, because two failures are both expensive and they are not symmetric. Coming back one day early risks the re-injury and a longer absence; keeping a player out one extra week risks fitness, form and morale unnecessarily. The honest answer is never a date, it is a sequence: every step is earned against a criterion, and the medical lead decides when the criteria are met, not the fixture list.

## The progression ladder, rung by rung

Walk before jog: the first rung is pain-free walking, then pain-free jogging at a talking pace, then progressive running — acceleration, deceleration, changes of direction, all pain-free. Then sport-specific work: technical drills at low intensity, then contact-naïve or non-contact roles, then full training with the squad, then a bench appearance, then managed minutes. Each rung is a gate, not a suggestion. A player who re-injures at rung three skipped rungs one and two, and the load that broke the muscle the first time will break it again at exactly the same pace. The gate most often skipped is the change-of-direction rung — it feels different from straight-line running, and it is where the hamstring's eccentric brake is actually tested.

## Criteria, not calendars

Every rung should be cleared by a test, not a Tuesday. Strength criteria typically ask the injured limb to produce comparable force to the uninjured one on a hamstring curl or a Nordic lowering, often with a target like 90 percent of the other side, and by objective running metrics — top speed, repeated sprints, deceleration counts — rather than time in rehab alone. Clinicians use isokinetic, handheld dynamometer or NordBord-style testing where it exists; in amateur setups, a hop test battery and honest GPS numbers are better than nothing and far better than a calendar. The criterion language matters at a board level too: the medical lead signs a player fit; the head coach signs him into the plan.

## The weeks nobody sees

The highest re-injury risk sits in the first two weeks back, and it is not mystical — it is load. After the detraining window, the muscle's force absorption capacity lags the player's self-perceived readiness by days to weeks, so the first sessions back must be engineered, not improvised. Progressive overload applies to rehab the way it applies to pre-season: volume up, then high-speed running restored, then sprints, then contact, then minutes capped, then monitored. A player coming off a hamstring strain gets high-speed exposure back *gradually*; a player who returns to 90 minutes cold after five weeks is a statistic waiting to happen.

## Evidence and its limits

The rungs above are consistent with mainstream sports-medicine practice: pain-free walking → running → sport-specific → full training → managed return, each gated by strength and running criteria. Where the article is deliberately vague is on the *numbers* — no single percentage, distance, or week-count applies to every injury, because grade-one and grade-three strains, different muscles, different ages and different positions justify different criteria, and the least honest thing an article can do is print one threshold as law. The peer-reviewed literature broadly supports criterion-based progression over time-based return, and Nordic-exercise eccentric programmes as a preventive tool — but this page is coaching orientation, not a medical protocol, and any return decision belongs to the club's medical staff with the player's consent.

## When this does not apply

This page is not guidance for concussion, which has its own graduated-return protocols and none of this ladder applies to the head; it is not guidance for immediate post-surgery cases, which are surgeon-led; and it is not a substitute for a physio who has watched *this* player's footage. It is also not a tool for the fan on the sofa — guessing a rival's return date from a training photo is a hobby, not a diagnosis. For the academy environment, note the added wrinkle that an under-eighteen's growth plate questions belong with the medical team entirely, not with the head coach.
