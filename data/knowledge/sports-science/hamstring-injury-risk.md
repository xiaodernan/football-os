## Where the hamstring earns its money in a sprint

Ask why you pulled a hamstring while sprinting and the answer is that the muscle has a thankless job. In the late swing of each stride, just before the foot reaches forward to contact, the hamstrings contract while being lengthened, braking the lower leg. That lengthening-under-load action is demanding, and it happens at speed, without warning, dozens of times in a match. Longer, more aggressive strides and higher speeds increase the demand, which is why top-speed exposure is both the risk and, handled gradually, the protection.

## Risk drivers that show up in ordinary squads

The best-established risk factor is a previous hamstring injury, which should shape how carefully a returning player is progressed. Other drivers recur in practice: low sprint exposure, meaning weeks that never include fast running; spikes in volume or intensity after a break or a bench spell; fatigue late in a match or a microcycle; poor sprint mechanics and weak deceleration; limited hip control and low hip extensor strength; older age; plus tight schedules and travel. These are patterns across squads, not reliable predictions about one person on one afternoon, and plenty of injured players had none of them.

## Graded exposure: the practical protection

Because fast running is the demand the muscle must tolerate, tolerance is built by meeting it in small, repeatable doses rather than avoiding it. A typical build uses short accelerations to around 85 to 95 percent of perceived maximum over 20 to 40 metres, with full recovery between efforts, starting at perhaps six to eight contacts and adding a couple of efforts a week. Speed rises before volume does, and each step up should be repeatable the next day. Efforts stay short so nobody is chasing a maximal time, and the session ends while technique holds. Players who never sprint are not protected; they are unprepared for the stride that asks for it.

## Strength that matches the demand

Sprint exposure prepares the muscle at speed; strength work prepares it under load, and most programmes use both. The useful exercises load the hamstrings while the hip extends and the knee resists straightening, for modest sets of perhaps five to ten repetitions one or two times a week, progressed by adding load rather than endlessly adding reps. Bridge-style hip extension, single-leg variations and controlled slow lengthening belong here, with basic hip and trunk work so the pelvis stays stable while the legs move. These movements are uncomfortable and often taken close to fatigue, so build form and volume carefully.

## Common mistakes in building hamstring tolerance

- Only running at submaximal effort all season, then expecting match-speed strides to be safe. Correct by keeping a small weekly dose of fast running.
- Adding speed, volume and strength in the same week. Correct by changing one variable at a time.
- Sprinting through a change in sensation in one leg. Correct by ending the session.
- Cutting the build short after two good weeks. Correct by holding each step long enough to repeat it.
- Ignoring deceleration, which loads the muscle in a different way. Correct by practising controlled braking.
- Doing only gym work and no running, or only running and no strength. The muscle needs both.

## After a tight or sore hamstring

A twinge during running deserves a conservative response: stop the session, keep moving gently as comfort allows, and rebuild tolerance gradually rather than testing it the next day with a maximal stride. Returning to sprinting is done by stages, easy running first, then accelerations, then true speed, with each stage repeated on more than one day. The player who resumes full speed as soon as pain settles is usually the one who reports back three weeks later with the same complaint. I cannot diagnose what you felt: a sudden snap with bruising, pain that stops you running, or repeated episodes on one side are reasons to be assessed by a qualified medical professional.

## Evidence and its limits

Say plainly: much of the detail here is general practice rather than settled science. Beyond previous injury, individual predictors are weak, which is why no screening test can promise to identify who will pull a hamstring. Research on Nordic-style hamstring strengthening is the most cited evidence in this area and is broadly favourable, but reported effects vary between studies and settings, the quality of that evidence has been debated, and translating laboratory protocols into a real season is not straightforward. Stretching before sprinting has not shown a clear protective effect. I have deliberately avoided quoting figures, naming studies or attributing findings, and every number above is a plausible coaching range rather than a measured result.

## When this does not apply

Not everyone should be building toward sprint exposure, and this framing assumes a healthy adult with no active symptoms. Anyone currently injured, recently returned, or feeling pain that changes their gait should follow a qualified medical professional's plan instead of a progression. Youth players need far smaller doses of genuine maximal speed, with tolerance built mostly through games and varied movement rather than structured sprint repetitions. Very hot conditions, unfamiliar surfaces or a heavy travel week are reasons to lower the dose rather than follow the plan. Off-field running for fitness is a different goal and needs no near-maximal efforts.
