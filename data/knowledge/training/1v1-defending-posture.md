## How to stop getting beaten in 1v1: the honest answer is patience

Anyone asking how to stop getting beaten one v one is usually diving, reaching or standing too close. The duel is rarely lost at the moment of the dribble; it is lost a second earlier when the defender commits his weight. The aim of defending 1v1 is not to win the ball on sight, it is to delay the attack, steer the attacker into ground where a teammate can help, and only then challenge when the odds are good. A defender who can slow an attacker down for two seconds has already done his job, because two seconds is enough for recovery runners and the goalkeeper to reorganise.

## The set position

Good posture here means a position you can move from in any direction. Feet roughly shoulder-width or a little wider, one foot slightly ahead of the other so you are never flat-footed, knees bent, hips lowered and turned enough to run backwards without looking at them. The torso stays tall rather than leaning in towards the ball, arms loose and available for balance, not flapping. Head up, eyes on the ball and the attacker's hips rather than his feet, because the hips betray direction before the feet do. A useful coaching cue is "sit on your hips", which mostly stops the common habit of standing upright with the knees locked.

## Distance: how far away to be

Distance is the variable defenders get wrong most often. Close enough that the attacker cannot see space, far enough that he cannot beat you with one touch. A typical starting distance against an attacker moving at speed is about an arm and a half to two arm lengths, tightening as your support arrives or as he slows. Full sprint towards you means backpedal and open up the gap. He stops, or takes a heavy touch, is your cue to close. The rule of thumb: shorten the distance only when the ball is less dangerous than it was a moment ago, never because you feel like you should be closer.

## When to jockey and when to challenge

Jockeying means containing rather than winning: half-turn your body to show the attacker one side, funnel him towards the touchline or towards a teammate, and keep matching his steps with short, quick ones. Challenge when one of four things is true. He takes a touch too long or too wide. He is close to the touchline with no escape. You have support behind or beside you so a beaten duel is not a break. Or the ball has settled and you are balanced while he is on one foot. If none of those apply, contain and wait, because a lunge that misses costs you the game's most expensive thing: a defender on the ground.

## Common mistakes and the correction

- Lunging at the first feint. Correct by making the attacker take three touches before you attempt a challenge.
- Cross-stepping while backing off, which traps the hips. Correct by side-shuffling until you can turn and run.
- Standing square and flat-footed. Correct by keeping one foot loaded and never letting the knees lock.
- Watching the feet and the ball only. Correct by looking at the hips and the space behind you in the same glance.
- Retreating in a straight line, which concedes the shot. Correct by backing towards the goal-side line, not away from it.
- Trying to win every duel. Correct by counting a two-second delay as a successful rep.

## A partner drill and a small-sided game

For the drill, mark a 12 metre channel. The attacker starts at one end dribbling towards you; you start 6 metres in, backpedal, contain and try to steer him out of one end zone rather than tackling. Typical dose is eight efforts per defender with a short walk-back recovery, then swap. Regress by giving the attacker a slow, feet-only dribble; progress by adding a second attacker so you must decide whether to hold or commit. For the game, play 2v2 or 3v3 in a 20 by 15 metre area with mini goals, and award the defending team a point for forcing a turnover outside the attacking third. Two defenders touching the attacker before a shot is another simple scoring rule that rewards delay.

## When this does not apply

Some situations are not 1v1 contests and should not be defended as if they were. Inside your own penalty area against a player with his back turned, the correct action is often to show him the sideline and get goal-side rather than hunt the ball, because a foul there is a penalty. If a teammate is already closing from the other side, holding your position and covering the pass beats joining the duel, since two defenders chasing one attacker creates the gap nobody picks up. With a fresh attacker against a tired defender late in a game, patience and fouling tactically may be the honest options. And if a knee or ankle is unstable or painful, that is a question for a qualified medical professional, not something posture coaching can solve.
