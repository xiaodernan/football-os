## Why your passes get intercepted when a press arrives

Players who ask why their passing falls apart under pressure almost always have a passing problem that is not about pressure. When an opponent is close, three things quietly change: the window is smaller, so weight and angle matter far more; the receiver has less time, so a lazy pass becomes a turnover; and the passer's own eyes narrow onto the ball instead of the lane. Under a slow, unopposed pass the same technique looks fine because error is forgiven. The fix is to practise with the constraint in place, so weight, angle and the decision to use two touches are trained together rather than separately.

## Weight: firm, on the ground, to the correct foot

Weight is what most people mean by "a good pass". A firm, low pass arriving at foot height is easier to control than a floating one, because float gives the ball time to drop badly and gives defenders time to read it. Aim for the receiver's better foot, or the foot pointing into the space he wants to use, and lead him away from a trailing defender rather than passing to where he is standing. A pass into a runner's path should be struck hard enough that he does not have to slow down. General coaching practice: if the receiver has to break stride or reach behind him, the pass was under-weighted for the distance, and the fault is the passer's, not the receiver's.

## Angle: pick the lane that still exists a second from now

An angle is only good if it survives the moment of delivery. A pass through a lane that a pressor can cover in one step is a coin flip, so look for lanes defended by someone further away, or create a lane by moving your own body first. Two habits help. Passing across the defender's body rather than around his nose, because turning and re-adjusting costs him more than you lose. And passing to a teammate's far side when a marker is on his near side, which puts your pass and his body between the ball and the opponent. When every lane is covered, the correct pass is usually backwards or sideways, buying tempo rather than territory.

## The two-touch game and why it beats a press

Two touches, receive then play, is the cheapest way to break a press because it removes the second where a presser gets closest. The first touch sets direction away from pressure, the second delivers. It also forces a decision before the ball arrives, since there is no time to think after it lands. The trap is one-touch heroics: attempting a first-time pass through a covered lane, or holding the ball too long hoping for a better option. Where a press is organised, one-touch play works when it is planned in sequence with teammates. Where the press is chaotic, two-touch keeps possession and resets the angle. Learn when you are in which situation, and be willing to take the touch.

## Common mistakes and the correction

- Scooped, floating passes. Correct by striking through the middle of the ball with the laces area, follow-through towards the target.
- Plant foot pointing away from the target. Correct by setting the toe where you want the ball to go.
- Passing to the receiver's standing foot with a defender there. Correct by naming the foot you are aiming at before you play.
- Looking at the ball at contact. Correct by checking the target as the ball leaves your foot.
- Only ever the safe option. Correct by giving every player two permitted line-breaking passes per small-sided game.
- Receiving then hunting for a pass. Correct by deciding the destination during the ball's flight.

## A wall routine and a pressured rondo

Solo: stand 8 to 10 metres from a wall with a rebound, set two cones 1 metre either side of your standing position as targets. Two touches only, receive and strike the rebound into a target, for three sets of 20 with 60 seconds rest, alternating feet on the second set. Partner version: a 4v2 rondo in a 10 by 10 square, two touches maximum, where the two inside players try to intercept. Typical structure is eight 60-second bouts. Progress by moving to a touch limit of one for the outside players facing a bigger press, or by adding a third pressor and widening the square; regress by allowing three touches and shrinking the press to one player.

## When this does not apply

Passing is the wrong answer when the space in front is already there. If a teammate is beaten and a direct run or a shot is on, dribbling or shooting beats recycling possession, and insisting on the safe pass throws away the attack. Long balls over a high press are a legitimate tactic, not a failed short pass, particularly against a press that commits every player forward. Set pieces in the opposition half sometimes call for a cleared header rather than a composed pass on the edge of the box. On a boggy pitch, a driven ground pass into traffic is worse than a weighed lofted ball into space. And if an ankle or foot injury makes striking painful, this is a matter for a qualified medical professional rather than a technical adjustment.
