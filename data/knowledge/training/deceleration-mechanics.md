## Why you lose balance when you stop or change direction

Players searching for why they fall over when they stop usually describe the same thing: they arrive at the cone on two flat feet, the upper body keeps travelling, and the legs have to chase the torso to stay upright. Deceleration is a skill with its own mechanics, and most training programmes rehearse acceleration and almost never rehearse braking. Slowing down means putting force into the ground against your direction of travel, repeatedly, while staying balanced enough to choose the next action. If you cannot brake well, you cannot press, recover, cut inside or arrive at a ball under control, which is why an attacker who looks "clumsy at speed" is often simply a poor decelerator.

## The mechanics of a good brake

Effective braking is a series of short, powerful steps rather than one big stopping step. Each extra foot contact spreads the work over more time and more muscle, which keeps the centre of mass over the base of support. The key actions: lower the hips by bending the knees and ankles rather than hinging at the waist; get the feet under the body instead of reaching ahead; keep the trunk tall but slightly forward so the hips stay over the middle foot; and use the arms to counterbalance rather than letting them go still. A useful cue is "take the steps out, don't lean back", because leaning backwards moves the mass behind the feet and guarantees a stumble.

## Foot contact and the last three steps

In the final three steps before a stop or a cut, foot contact shifts from a midfoot strike under the knee to a firmer, flatter placement slightly ahead, with the ankle prepared to absorb. Think of the foot landing almost parallel rather than pointed hard outward, so the knee tracks over the second and third toes instead of collapsing inwards. Width matters too: a narrow, crossing-step base gives you nothing to push against, so the last step should be wide enough to feel the ground under the whole foot. Players who "roll over" an ankle when stopping frequently arrive with one leg crossed behind the other and no lateral support.

## Common mistakes and the correction

- One huge stopping step. Correct by drilling a fixed count of three quick steps into every stop.
- Reaching with straight legs, which brakes with the heel and jars the knee. Correct by cuing "feet under the hips".
- Hinging forward from the waist until the head is over the ball. Correct by keeping shoulders back over hips.
- Locking the arms. Correct by giving the arms a job: elbows bent, driving opposite to the legs.
- Turning the foot out hard to pivot. Correct by practising the same turn with the hips, knees and toes aligned.
- Only practising at half speed. Correct by building to near-match speed, since balance fails at speed, not at a jog.

## A solo braking drill with reps

Mark two cones 15 metres apart. Build to a strong run over the first five metres, then take three fast steps and stop balanced inside a two-metre zone beside the second cone, held for two seconds without a recovery step. Walk back, and repeat facing the other way so you learn to brake to both sides. A typical dose is three sets of six stops per direction with a full walk-back recovery, so the drill stays about quality rather than conditioning. Regress by shortening the approach to 8 metres or by allowing a small hop to settle. Progress by adding a cue: on a partner's call, stop and immediately sprint 5 metres in a new direction, or stop and receive a pass so the next action is decided under control.

## Putting it into a small-sided game

Play 3v3 or 4v4 with a stop line 5 metres in front of each mini goal: an attacker must have both feet inside the zone, or come fully to a balance-held stop, before shooting. That rule creates dozens of genuine high-speed decelerations per player per session without any extra running load, because everyone has to brake to receive, shoot and defend. Six bouts of three minutes with one minute rest is a workable typical structure. To reduce the demand, widen the zone; to raise it, shrink the zone and shorten the rest, and notice whether the technique collapses as the players tire, which is the most useful coaching information the game gives you.

## When this does not apply

Braking mechanics should not be drilled hard when someone is already very sore, or at the end of a session when fatigue has ruined the quality of the stop and you are simply rehearsing poor positions. Younger players need far less volume than adults, and speed work for youth profiles should stay modest and game-shaped rather than maximal. If a hamstring, knee or ankle has recently been injured or hurts during high-effort running, return decisions belong to a qualified medical professional and not to a drill progression. Players with heavy, uneven or wet surfaces underfoot should shorten the approach rather than trust a sharp stop. And in a match situation, a controlled stop is sometimes the wrong choice: if a defender is closing on your hip, a first-time pass or a change of pace without stopping is often the better answer than an elegant brake.
