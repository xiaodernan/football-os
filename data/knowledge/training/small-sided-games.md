## "what does a 4v4 actually teach my under-15s"

Small-sided games — 3v3, 4v4, 5v5, with a keeper or without — are the format where football coaching stops talking and starts happening. Every real game mechanic is compressed: decision density is enormous, touches per player are multiplied, and the space shrinks to the exact shape where the next action is always urgent. A good SSG is not a smaller version of an eleven; it is a microscope pointed at the same game.

## Why the format works

The math is simple. In a small grid, each player sees the ball more often (more reps per minute), faces pressure more often (every opponent is close), and must decide more often (the ball outruns thought only when there is space to hide in). Pitch size acts as a difficulty dial: smaller pitch, less time, less space, more decisions; larger pitch, more time to organise. Numerical imbalance acts as a bias dial: underloaded teams get pressed into faster circulation — a 4v5 forces the outnumbered side to find the free man — while overloaded teams learn how to hurt a compact block. Those two dials, pitch and numbers, are most of the coaching vocabulary of SSGs.

## Constraints are the curriculum

Rules are not decoration, they are the lesson. "Two touches only" trains receiving and releasing under pressure; "score only from a through ball" trains line-breaking passes; "goals count double after a direct free kick" trains set-piece urgency; "the wide players may not leave the channel" trains width discipline. Constraints should be visible to players, explainable in one sentence, and removed once the behaviour is stable — a permanent constraint teaches a permanent crutch. The classic error is the opposite: so many constraints stacked that the game becomes a rule quiz and the football disappears.

## Dosage and formats

Typical session design uses SSGs as the main course, not the warm-up: briefed, competitive, recorded, and debriefed. Rough reps — two or three bouts of three to six minutes, with real rest between — are the norm rather than a single long block; decision quality collapses when the brain is on fumes. In matchday-minus-one sessions, SSGs stay intensity-matched to keep touch sharpness; in building-week sessions they carry the tactical theme. For academy age groups, the ball size, pitch dimensions and scoring are scaled, and the football is played flat-out but fun — a format where nobody scores is a detention, not a drill.

## Measuring what the format produced

Effect on behaviour shows up in the numbers coaches can already collect: passes per sequence, forward attempts, successful pressures per minute, turnovers in each third, and — most honestly — the clip of the exact situation the SSG was designed to create. A 6v6 with a narrow middle should end with the coach able to pull three or four clips showing either the compression working or the pin-ball collapse of the plan. If the metric moves but the clips don't show it, the metric was noise; if the clips move and the metric doesn't, count better next week.

## Evidence and its limits

The evidence base for SSGs is consistent on the direction of effect — more touches, more decisions, higher intensity per minute, better technical execution under pressure at typical academy and amateur dosages — and honest reviews note wide variability in how studies measure "performance", with much of the literature centred on youth populations and short-term outcomes. Exact heart-rate zones and percentage improvements differ by age, pitch and fitness; the ranges here are coaching orientation, not physiological law. What the format does not do by itself is create a pressing system — it creates the decision-making *inside* one.

## When this does not apply

SSGs do not replace real matches, and they do not teach eleven-a-side structure — spacing in a low block, build-up lanes, rest-defence shape — at all. A team that only trains SSGs will press well in 4v4 and stand two metres too wide on Saturday. Older senior squads with deeply ingrained habits may need full-size phase work more than another grid; tired legs late in a season may need lower-intensity technical patterns rather than an all-out 4v4; and a painful knee does not care how fun the small pitch is. Use SSGs to sharpen the small decisions; use the eleven when the decision is about the team.
