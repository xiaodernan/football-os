## What scanning is actually for

A midfielder asking how to see the field before the ball arrives is asking the right question, because the difference between a composed player and a panicked one is usually information gathered before contact, not foot speed after it. Scanning is not looking around prettily. It is a short, deliberate check of what you cannot see with your eyes forward, taken while the ball is elsewhere, so that when it reaches you the decision is a recall rather than a discovery. The payoff in ordinary language: fewer bad first touches, fewer passes into covered lanes, fewer turns that end with a defender on your back.

## Four things to look for, in order

When you check over your shoulder, look for four items in a fixed order so the scan becomes repeatable. First, where is the nearest pressure, and which side is it coming from. Second, where is the space that pressure has just created. Third, which teammate occupies that space, or which lane leads to it. Fourth, where you are standing relative to the passer and the marker, because your position may already answer the question. Four items in a set order beats a general glance, and it stops the common failure of checking and then acting on nothing. If you only ever have time for one item, take the first one: the direction the pressure is arriving from decides everything else.

## When to check: timing inside a possession sequence

The most productive moments to scan are the seconds when the ball is not on you. As the ball leaves your teammate for someone else, you get a free look. As it travels towards you, you get a second look, and this one should refresh rather than repeat, because opponents move. A defender's position two seconds ago is a hypothesis, not a fact. Practically, that means two checks per possession cycle: one while the ball travels elsewhere, one while it travels to you. Players who scan only after receiving gather information too late to use it, and players who scan once and stop often turn into exactly the marker they saw a moment before.

## Turning information into an action

A scan that does not change your body shape or your touch is decoration. The link is worth naming out loud in training: pressure from the left means receive on the right hip and take the first touch away from it; an empty lane ahead means a touch that carries and a run that goes; a teammate free between the lines means your check should be timed to his pass, not to your comfort. Decide one of three options before the ball arrives, typically turn, lay off, or hold and recycle, so the first touch is chosen by the plan. Coaches often phrase this as "scan, name it, shape for it", which keeps the chain of behaviour short enough to use at match speed.

## Common mistakes and the correction

- Checking without acting. Correct by requiring the player to say out loud where the pressure is before receiving.
- Only looking to the ball side. Correct by cueing the far shoulder, where the dangerous player usually is.
- Glancing with the eyes while the head stays still. Correct by turning the whole head, chin towards the shoulder.
- Scanning at the wrong moment, right as the ball arrives. Correct by attaching the check to a trigger, such as the pass leaving.
- Freezing while checking. Correct by keeping the feet active and moving into the pass during the look.
- Never holding the picture. Correct by pausing the game and asking a player to describe the last thing he saw.

## Two drills with reps

Solo or paired with little equipment: a partner stands behind you and raises one to four fingers while you face a ball at your feet. On a trigger word you check, report the number, then receive a pass and play it to the cone matching that number. Three sets of ten with 60 seconds rest, about ten minutes. Game version: in a 4v2 or 5v2 rondo the receiver must name where the nearest pressure is as the ball arrives, and a missing or wrong answer means the next pass must go backwards. Eight bouts of 90 seconds. Regress by allowing three touches so there is time to look; progress by shrinking the square or adding a pressor.

## When this does not apply

Scanning has diminishing returns once the decision is obvious. Sixty metres from goal with no one near, look at the ball and run. Deep in your own box under a pressing trap, a first-time pass to a rehearsed option beats waiting for a better picture, and over-checking can slow a break that was on. For players still building ball control, asking for a shoulder check while the first touch is unreliable overloads them; comfort first, then awareness. In fog or heavy rain the information itself is degraded, so plan safer options. And a scan is never an excuse for standing still and watching the game.
