## How to analyse a game without following the ball

Watching a match is not the same as reading one, and the difference is mostly where the eyes go. The ball is the least informative thing on the pitch, because by the time it arrives somewhere the decision has already been made. Players who get better at analysis watch the space around the ball and the shape of the eleven bodies not involved in it. A wide viewpoint helps enormously: seeing both penalty areas at once turns a chaotic scene into two competing structures, and the question becomes which structure is creating the better problem.

## In possession: ask what the plan is, not who is passing

Start with a blunt test. When your team has the ball and nobody is pressing them, where does it go? If the answer is always the same channel or the same player, there is a habit rather than a plan. Then ask how the ball travels from your own area into the opposition half, and whether that route is repeatable. Look at the gaps between the lines, because a team with eight players on one horizontal layer can circulate but cannot progress. Finally, ask what happens in the final third: does the move arrive with runners in the box, or die at the edge of the area because nobody made the second run.

## Out of possession: find the pressure and the gap it leaves

Defending is easier to read if you watch two things at once: where the team is trying to win the ball, and which player is unaccounted for. A high block that presses with five but leaves one midfielder unmarked is a plan with a hole in it, and the hole gets found within twenty minutes. Ask what triggers the press, and whether it fires together or in ones and twos. Ask how fast the team shifts across when the ball travels laterally. Then look behind the line: if the space in front of the keeper is repeatedly won by an attacker rather than a defender, somebody is dropping too deep for the shape above them.

## Transitions: the phases that predict what happens next

Most telling observations in a match occur in the three seconds after possession changes. When your team loses the ball, count how many players are close enough to contest it at once, and how many are already running forwards. When the opponent loses it, look for the same numbers in reverse, because a team that cannot counter-press usually concedes its chances from the counter. The moment after a goal and every set-piece delivery are transitions too, which is why goals so often arrive in clusters after a restart.

## Set pieces: the free information nobody uses

Corners, free kicks and throw-ins are rehearsed sequences, so they reveal a coach's thinking more honestly than open play. Watch the delivery target and the pattern repeats itself across the match. Watch who blocks, who attacks the near post, and who is left on the edge to win the second ball or to stop the break. Watch whether the defending team changes its instruction when the attacker moves short. Set pieces are also where a physically outmatched team can compete, so a side losing midfield often survives because its routine defending is organised.

## Game state and time: the same event means different things

A team holding a one-goal lead defends differently, and analysis that ignores the score is describing a different match from the one being played. Ask whether a high press in the last ten minutes is a plan or a gamble, and whether early compactness was deliberate caution or the opponent forcing it. Look for the point where legs decide tactics, usually when both blocks stretch and play becomes more direct. Also ask what a team does when it is winning: how it manages a throw-in deep in the opponent's half, and whether it can keep the ball under pressure.

## A repeating ten-minute checking routine

Rather than watching ninety minutes for a vague impression, check the same handful of things at fixed intervals. Who is receiving between the lines, and has that changed since the last check. Which full-back is being used more, and what the opposite winger is doing about it. How often the team in possession reaches the byline versus recycling backwards. Where fouls are being given away, since a defence that keeps conceding in wide areas is being turned. Whether a substitution has flipped a matchup. Ten minutes is short enough that patterns do not hide and long enough that one lucky moment is not a trend.

## Turning observations into a half-time message

Analysis is only useful if it survives the walk down the corridor. Players do not need a summary; they need one problem and one instruction, phrased as an action rather than a criticism. Not "we are losing the second ball" but "the six steps to the edge of the area before the cross is delivered". Not "they keep finding their left eight" but "the right centre-back comes with, and the near midfielder closes the inside lane". Keep a few notes on one page, chosen during the game rather than reconstructed afterwards, so the half-time message comes from what you saw rather than from whatever you happened to remember.
