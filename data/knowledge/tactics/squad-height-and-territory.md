## What squad height is, and what it is not

Squad height is the average distance of the eleven from their own goal — how high the whole team
sits, not just the back line. Coaches say "we were compact and high" when the defensive line, the
midfield and the forwards all occupy the opponent's half, so possession is played in territory the
team took rather than territory the opponent offered. A back line pushed up in front of a deep
midfield is not a high squad; it is a team with a hole in it. Height only means something when the
distance between the lines stays short while the whole block moves up.

## Territory is the reason to hold height

Ask why a team bothers to sit high and the answer is not pressing alone. It is the
distance the ball has to travel. When your midfield wins the ball twenty-five metres from the
opponent's goal rather than forty, every pass after the win is shorter, the defence is already
retreating, and the counter-press has support nearby because everyone is close together. Held height
also moves the second ball: a clearance from your striker's header lands in a zone your team
occupies, so possession continues rather than restarting on the opponent's terms.

## The price of height is the space behind

Space in behind does not disappear when you push up — it moves. A squad that holds forty metres of
its own goal is a squad that has handed the opponent a target to play into, which is why height has
to be paid for with either a keeper able to sweep outside the area or centre-backs whose recovery
speed covers the ground their positioning concedes. The same eleven cannot both compress the middle and
protect fifty metres behind them, so pick the risk you intend to defend.

Too high begins the moment the team cannot reset. Concretely: when the back line's height depends
on a single step-over pass rather than on the ball being safe, when the midfield cannot get back
inside a few seconds of a turnover, or when the forwards' pressing runs leave the first passing
lane open behind them. A reliable tell is the second phase of the opponent's attack — if the first
ball over the top is chased but the second is headed into a zone where only the opponent has
bodies, the squad is higher than its cover.

## Compression makes height survivable

The metric worth tracking is the vertical distance between the deepest outfield player and the
most advanced one, not the height itself. A squad holding a short vertical distance can slide up
and down as a unit, which means the pass into the middle is contested from two directions and the
forward can press with a teammate behind him rather than in front. Widen that gap instead and it
becomes the channel an opponent plays through the moment he wins the ball: one pass into the space
between a defence that is still high and a midfield that has not arrived, and the counter is in the
final third before the team can compress again. Compression comes from the defenders stepping into
the midfielder's space on the ball's third touch, and from the eight checking in instead of standing
still after a pass.

## How a team earns territory in the first place

Height is not a switch. Teams move up through sequences that make the ground safe: a wide
possession that pins an opponent back, a set-piece or throw-in inside the opponent's half, a won
second ball followed by a tactical foul. Each lets the back line take five metres without facing a
run in behind. The alternative — signalling the line up from the touchline — produces a high
defensive line with no ball pressure above it, which is the version that concedes chances.

## Height in possession and height out of it are different decisions

The same team usually holds less height without the ball than with it, and the honest question is
how far the difference runs. Out of possession a mid block sits deeper on purpose, because the
goal is to protect space between the lines and force play wide. In possession the squad should be
higher than the block, which is what makes the pitch feel shorter for the team with the ball.
Coaches who demand one height for both phases end up with a team that cannot keep the ball high and
cannot defend deep, so state which number belongs to which phase.

## Recovery runs, rests defence and the counter you will concede

Every held metre of territory is borrowed against the turnover. Rest defence — the two or three
players positioned to face the ball if it is lost — is what makes height affordable, and it works
only when those players sit between the ball and the danger rather than beside it. The counter that
beats a high squad is rarely long in the air; it is played on the ground into a midfielder left
unmarked because the overload took three players forward. Count bodies behind the ball before
committing the fourth.

## Evidence and its limits

Everything above is general coaching practice stated in metres and sequencing, not a published
finding. No study, xG threshold or league average is cited, and the distances mentioned here are
illustrative rather than targets. Squad height is easy to measure on a board and easy to misread in
a match: it says where the team was, not whether that was the right choice, which depends on the
opponent's route to goal and on how the team behaves after losing the ball.
