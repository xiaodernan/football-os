## How do I stretch a compact block?

Ask this question correctly and the answer is already half written: you cannot stretch a compact
block by passing around the front of it. A low block is compact because the defenders have chosen
a small horizontal span and a short vertical distance between their lines, so the space you want is
not in the middle anywhere. The gap between one defender and his nearest team-mate is the distance
a combination has to play through, which is why a tight block is broken by movement and by the
switch rather than by more passes in front of it. Width in attack is the only demand that forces
that block to expand against its own interests, because the further the ball travels sideways, the
further the nearest defender has to run to reach it, and the more ground the whole unit has to shift
to stay covered.

## What width actually means on the grass

Width is not wingers standing on the touchline. It is the horizontal distance between the two outside
players in the attacking shape, visible to the defence as a span they have to cover. A team
attacking across most of the pitch's width asks a compact block to slide several metres before
every pass, turning covering distance into a race between the ball and the defenders' legs. Two
players hugging the touchline while the rest crowd the middle produce no stretch — the shape is wide
on paper and narrow where it counts.

## The switch is where the stretch is earned

A block shifts as a unit, and a unit shifts slower than a ball. The value of width therefore shows
up in the switch: a driven or clipped pass from one touchline to the other that beats the shift
rather than arriving after it. That demands three things at once — the far-side player holding his
position instead of drifting towards the ball, a receiver who can hit the distance in one motion,
and a first touch on the far side that points the ball back into the field rather than out for a
throw-in. Held width without a switch is a slow sideways pass, and a slow sideways pass is exactly
what a defending block is built to absorb.

## Overload one side so the other becomes real

The most reliable way to make width pay is to make the near side more attractive than the far one.
Put the ball, a supporting midfielder and an overlapping full-back into one channel and the block
has to answer: it slides, it commits bodies, and the far channel empties. The player waiting there
is then one pass from a duel with a defender who arrived late. This is why the far winger often
does nothing for thirty seconds of possession — his inactivity is the plan, and his job is to stay
high and wide so the overload has somewhere to point.

## Width needs runners behind the block, not only beside it

Stretching sideways creates the gap; running into it converts the chance. When the outside player
receives with touchline behind him, the block's cover is the nearest centre-back stepping out, and
that step opens space between the defender and the goal. Two or three arriving runners — an eight
from the second row, a striker curving his run from the far side — turn a stretched block into a
broken one. Crossing into a defended six-yard box is where width usually dies, so the arriving
runner matters more than the number of players outside.

## Full-backs decide how much width you can afford

Holding width high costs players behind the ball, and that is a defensive decision as much as an
attacking one. A team that keeps both full-backs high needs centre-backs able to cover the space
behind them and a midfielder who stays rather than joining. A more cautious version sends one full-
back and holds the other, which keeps the span shorter but makes the switch harder to defend
because the resting defender has a job to do. Neither is correct in the abstract; the question is
which one survives the turnover you will eventually concede.

## When width stops working

Width is a means, and sometimes it is the wrong one. If the touchline receiver is doubled
consistently, the block has accepted the wide duel and is protecting the middle, so the answer is
the pass inside that the wide player was always threatening. If the pitch, the wind or the
receiver's passing range makes a switch unreliable, holding width only loses the ball in the corner.
And against a block that is narrow by design but slow to shift, the far post from a near-side cross
is often a shorter route to the same goal than a switch across forty metres of grass.

The failures that make width look ineffective are usually not the opponent's doing: the wide player
drifting inside as the ball approaches, which quietly deletes the stretch; passing sideways at a
tempo the block can shuffle to; three players crowding one channel so the overload has no extra
body; a cross taken with no runner at the near post, far post or edge of the area.

## Evidence and its limits

This is general coaching practice about geometry and covering distance, not a finding. Nothing here
quotes a study, a possession-value model or a specific team's numbers, and no typical range should
be read as a threshold. The pitch is a fixed shape, so metre spans on it are arithmetic; judgements
about which overload, which switch, and which risk to accept depend on your players' passing range,
the opponent's shifting discipline and the score, none of which this page can see for you.
