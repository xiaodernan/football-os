## What a high press actually asks of you

Anyone asking how to get out of a high press should start by naming what the press is trying to do. A high block narrows the space behind your own midfield and crowd your defensive line, so the press is not really hunting the ball carrier. It is hunting the space you would pass into, and it is trying to make your first pass a gamble. The bet the pressing team places is that you will eventually panic, hit a low-percentage ball, or hand them a throw-in or a shot close to your goal. Beat that bet and the same structure leaves them with long distances to run back, which is why escaping a press is worth more than a safe clearance.

## The goalkeeper is part of the build-up, not a spectator

When eleven outfield players are pressed, the goalkeeper is the extra body that turns a flat line into an overload. That does not mean the keeper stands on the edge of the area waiting for a pass. It means positioning to offer a genuine angle, usually deeper and wider than the centre-backs expect, holding a stance that says the short option is real. A keeper who is visible but not usable is worse than one who stays on the line, because the pressing forward can crowd the passing lane at no cost. The first job is being on the correct side of the ball so the pass is playable at foot height, not chest height.

## Front, back and side: the three routes through the first line

There are only three directions out of a crowded first line. Through the front means a line-breaking pass into a midfielder who has turned. Through the back means a ball over or behind the pressing forwards into a full-back or a wide defender who has stayed high and stayed free. Around the side means using touchline width so the press has to travel further than the ball. Good teams do not prefer one of these; they sequence them. A pass backwards to the keeper, then sideways to a full-back, then through to the eight, is three moves of the same plan rather than three separate decisions.

## The third man: passing through a teammate instead of to him

Third-man play answers a simple problem: the pass you want is defended, but the pass after it is not. If a centre-back cannot reach a midfielder because a pressor is standing in the lane, but that midfielder can reach a wide teammate who is free, then the ball goes to the midfielder and immediately back out to the free player. The midfielder is the third man's bridge, and his first touch must point him towards where the ball is going next, not where it came from. Calling for a lay-off rather than a driven pass keeps the ball moving at the tempo the press cannot follow.

## Shape creates the pass, not courage

Players pressed hard often blame nerves, but most build-up failures are geometric. Passing lanes exist when bodies are split: staggered depths so no single pressor can cover two receivers, and enough width that the pressing midfield has to cover more ground than they can close. If three teammates stand on one horizontal line, whoever holds the ball has only lateral options and the press can shuffle across. Pairing a deep receiver with a checking runner in the same vertical corridor gives the carrier a choice between two markers rather than a coin flip.

## Reading which press is coming

Before the ball is played, look at what the opposition pressing shape is committing to. A press that follows man-for-man leaves space behind the pressors when a runner drags them, so the answer is movement, not extra passing. A press that holds a zone and protects the middle invites wide possession, so the answer is patience out there and a cut-back into the arriving midfielder. A press that funnels you one way is a trap, and the tell is usually a forward shading his body to show you the touchline. Spotting the trap early is what makes an escape look effortless.

## The five most common build-up failures

The same mistakes repeat at almost every level. Players standing still after passing instead of re-offering. Two teammates occupying the same lane so the carrier has fewer options than the shape suggests. Passing backwards the moment pressure arrives, which surrenders territory without a shot or a turnover. Turning under pressure when a simple outward pass to the keeper would have reset the angle. And a clearance taken in panic that gives away a good possession for nothing, when a controlled exit usually keeps the ball in the opposition half.
