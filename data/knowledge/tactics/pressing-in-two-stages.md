## The short answer

Modern pressing operates in two stages: the first wave is the trigger — the moment the ball moves into a shape that lets the press step across the sideline. The second wave is the trap — the moment the ball actually lands and the closing lane is shut. Confuse the two, and the press either arrives a half-second late or half a step early. Confront the geometry — first wave sits wide and forces the pass; second wave comes in from the inside and wins the second ball. The pattern itself is simple; the execution of the studs between the two is the whole craft.

## Why split the press into two stages

1. **Acknowledged within the team** — the first bank cannot run to the second bank without stepping out too early, and the wide forward — who pins the touchline — cannot chase without releasing the touchline's width. The two banks have fundamentally different jobs.
2. **Cognitive bandwidth** — one press bank cannot track both sides of the ball; splitting the decision keeps each player's visual bandwidth on one thing at a time.
3. **Risk discipline** — the first wave is a hint; the second wave is the commitment. Wrong timing in the first wave costs nothing; wrong timing in the second costs the press entirely.

## What the two-stage press concedes

It concedes nothing when it works at all (the second wave wins the ball). It concedes exactly the wide-out shot to the touchline, which is a shot at a tight angle with a full back coming back on, or a back-pedallng panic when the press triggers correctly from deeper.

## Where the two-stage press breaks

- If the first-wave player sprints before the trigger — the trap never sets, and the wide player gets free width.
- If the second-wave player commits too late (when the receiver hasn't even faced the ball) — the trap never snaps shut.
- If the second wave steps out and the wide wide player pin-drags toward touchline — the depth route opens.
