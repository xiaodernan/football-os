## The first five seconds decide most counters

The moment the ball is lost is the moment most goals against are set up. In the first few seconds after a turnover, the team that has just lost possession is stretched, facing forward, and mentally still in attack, while the team that won it is compact, facing forward, and released. Everyone asks what to do in possession; the more valuable question is what happens immediately after losing the ball, because that window is short, predictable and trainable. Nothing complicated is required in those seconds. Deny the space behind, make the opponent play sideways, and give the rest of the team time to get back.

## What a counter-press is for

A counter-press is not the same as pressing well in general. It is an immediate, coordinated hunt by the players nearest the ball, launched the instant possession changes. It has three possible prizes, and only one of them is winning the ball back. The first is a clean recovery in a dangerous area. The second is forcing a hurried long ball that your centre-backs can compete for. The third, and the one coaches underrate, is simply slowing the game down so the players behind you can retreat and rebuild the block. Any of those three is a success; the failure is a scattered press where three players chase the carrier and nobody covers the pass.

## The nearest player: press the carrier on the correct side

The first defender's job is to delay, not to win. Body position does most of the work. Coming in on the side of the carrier removes one direction and forces the head up, which buys a second or two more than a head-on challenge that can be skipped with a feint. The practical instruction is to arrive at a speed that lets you stop: a player sprinting at full pace cannot change direction when the ball is moved. Curve the approach, show one side, and stay balanced enough to react if the opponent drops the shoulder and goes the other way.

## The second defender: cover the pass, not the player

The most common counter-press failure is two players attacking the ball. The second closest teammate should be positioned on the line between the carrier and the most likely receiver, usually the player behind the press in midfield. That is the outlet pass every counter depends on. The instruction is not to mark anyone but to shadow the lane, which turns the carrier's obvious option into a risky one. A third player then holds slightly deeper to catch the second pass, and the shape becomes a small triangle rather than a crowd.

## Rest defence: what good looks like before the ball is lost

Transition defending is mostly decided before the turnover. Rest defence is the term for how the players not involved in the attack are arranged while the team is still in possession. A sensible shape leaves bodies in the central corridor behind the ball, staggered so no single pass splits two players at once, and close enough to the losing area that they can pressure the counter within a few strides. If an attack sends six or seven players forward with nobody held back, the counter-press has no foundation and the recovery run becomes a footrace against tired legs.

## When the counter is already on: protect the middle

If the opponent has escaped the first press, the objective changes from winning to delaying, and the priority is the central lane. Recovery runs should bend towards your own goal and towards the middle, herding the carrier towards the touchline where the angle of attack shrinks. Track runners rather than the ball, because a counter is finished by a second player arriving, not by the first one dribbling. Communication matters more here than fitness: somebody has to say who jumps, who covers, and who drops, or two defenders leave a striker free in the space between them.

## Tactical fouls, risk and the referee

When a counter cannot be stopped cleanly, players sometimes pull the opponent's shirt or nick the ball from behind to halt the break. This is a calculated risk that belongs to the player making the decision and to the rules of the competition, and the trade-off is clear: a booking now instead of a shot later. Coaches should still be honest about the cost. A caution early in a match narrows a player's options afterwards, and a reckless challenge near the touchline can be worse than the counter it prevented. Standing on the last line and showing an opponent your feet is safer than a lunge that leaves you in the dressing room.

## Habits worth drilling

Counter-pressing improves with repetition far faster than most tactical ideas, because it is triggered by one event that training can produce on demand. Small-sided games where losing the ball means instantly hunting it teach the reflex. Add a rule that the first touch after a turnover must be contested within two seconds and players begin to look at their own positioning when their team has the ball, which is exactly the awareness that makes the first five seconds survivable.
