## The four states a match can be in

Strip away the noise and a match has exactly four states. Your team has the ball (in
possession). Their team has the ball (out of possession, from your side). The ball has just
changed hands — a window measured in seconds, not minutes (transition). And the ball is dead
and being restarted (a set piece). Every tactical instruction on a team sheet is a rule for
what to do in one of these states, and the useful way to think about a game is not "we pressed
them high" but "we spent the match in our out-of-possession state, and we won the transitions
inside it". A state machine mindset makes admitting that easier, because states stop being
abstractions and start being places the match can go.

## Transitions are where matches are decided

The most dangerous seconds in football are the ones between states. When your team loses the
ball, eleven minds switch from attack to defence a half-second late; when your team wins it,
the opponent's shape is still broken. A typical league range, offered here as general
observation rather than a finding: counter-attacking moves and shots conceded from turnovers
account for roughly a third to a half of goals in top divisions. What matters more than the
exact share is the asymmetry — transitions are inevitable in every match, so the team that
trains them deliberately wins them more often than the team that hopes pressing solves
everything. The first five seconds after a regain or a loss decide the next phase.

## In-possession states: build-up, progression, final third

In possession is not one state but three. Build-up: the ball is in areas where a mistake is
costly because it backs onto your own goal — the problem is finding safe angles, not
inventing genius. Progression: the ball is between the lines and decisions get faster; the
job is to beat one line at a time, not two. Final third: the block is set, space is scarce,
and the state tolerates fewer passes and sharper runs. Teams that pretend all in-possession
minutes are the same state end up where weak team-building build-ups are played at the same
tempo as box attacks, and the whole attack collapses at the first press. A coach asking "how
did we lose it?" should be given the state's answer: build-up error, progression error or
final-third error — three different problems with three different cures.

## Out-of-possession states: who owns the ball changes the map

Out of possession is one state entered three ways — high block, mid block, low block — and
choosing which is a statement about risk. The high block buys territory at the cost of one
bypass; the mid block holds the middle of the pitch and accepts its own half; the low block
concedes territory and refuses the box. The failure mode in all three is a state nobody
recognises: the team that press-hopes halfway between a high and mid block, i.e. nobody on
the pitch quite knows the trigger, so the shape is eleven individuals' opinions. A state
machine framing forces the conversation: "we are in the mid block now — who presses, who
drops, who covers?" The answer differs by state and nobody should be guessing it mid-match.

## Transition states: the two that decide the rest

There are exactly two transition states and they are not mirror images. After winning the
ball: transition to attack — first ball, first support, first run; the rest of the shape may
take five seconds and that is fine if the first three are right. After losing it: transition
to defence — the immediate counter-press before retreating, then rest defence (the
substitution of one attacker for a covering defender) so a counter does not find an open lane
before the press sets. Most teams drill both as "transition" and half the value is lost;
they are different jobs. The first asks run forward; the second asks three players to own a
5-second window in which nobody recovers position.

## Set pieces are their own machine

A set piece is a state where the transition phase is suspended — the ball stopped, the shape
known, the situation rehearsed in training rather than improvised on match night. Treating
corners as chaotic wastes that suspension: a rehearsed near-post routine is worth more than a
thousand open-play crosses. Teams that assign one owner to each of the four states (in, out,
transition-to-attack, transition-to-defence) and one owner to the set-piece state end the
discussion every match does not need to have.

## Training the state machine on the pitch

Conditioned games are the honest rehearsal: stop the game on any turnover and restart from
there alone; score only transitions; make the first five seconds after a loss worth a point.
The players learn what each state smells like, and the coach stops correcting eleven
individuals and starts coaching one system. Video analysis of the same transitions — regained
then lost within six seconds — closes the loop, and a report can print the arithmetic: how
many transitions, how many converted, which state leaks.

## When this does not apply

With youth players before puberty, letting every child experience every state with the ball
billed high is age-appropriate; drilling phases of play into under-eights is not. It also
does not excuse poor technique, and it cannot rescue a side that loses every duel — no schema
fixes that. It is a map, not a substitute for legs, touch and courage.
